## Before Tempo: spreadsheets

At Weinmann Technologies, working time was logged in Excel files. No formal approval, no traceability, no overview for managers, and absences copied by hand from the HR tool.

I built Tempo solo during my work-study program, front end and back end.

## Three roles, one approval flow

The heart of Tempo isn't data entry, it's the **flow**: who logs, who approves, who corrects, and the record of all of it.

## Absences show up on their own

Absences lived in **Silae**, the HR tool. Instead of re-entering them, a Python script syncs them into Tempo's database automatically:

```
Silae (HR)  ──►  Python script  ──►  MySQL  ──►  Tempo
```

The result: employees see their leave already filled in when they log their week, and managers approve against accurate data.

The tricky part was understanding Silae's own logic and avoiding **duplicates** on every sync. Approval, for its part, has to absorb the exceptions of a real calendar: public holidays, paid leave, long absences.

## Fixed-days contracts

Some managers in France work on a *forfait jours*: they count days per month, not hours. They had no suitable tool; Tempo gives them dedicated tracking of their monthly allowance, alongside the regular time entry.

## What changed

<Callout title="In production">
Tempo is used every day across the whole company. The spreadsheets are gone for good, and approval saves managers a significant amount of time.
</Callout>

The tool keeps evolving with user feedback, which is probably the best sign it found its place.

## Stack and deployment

HTML and TailwindCSS for the interface, Node.js / Express and MySQL on the server, served by PM2 behind an Nginx reverse proxy.
